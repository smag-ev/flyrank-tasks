01

Title & Abstract

Refresh Priority Scoring: an honest comparison of a heuristic rule and a Logistic Regression model for prioritizing content-decay review queues.

Which published pages should an editorial team refresh first, to stop quiet organic-search decay before it compounds? This paper builds and evaluates a prioritization score on 30,000 anonymized articles across 32 sites, comparing a hand-tuned heuristic rule against a Logistic Regression model trained on five decision-time-safe features, under a client-grouped, leakage-checked validation split. Against a pre-registered target of >85% Precision@50 (set before modeling began), the heuristic baseline reached 100%, while the honest model reached 52% — a gap traced directly to the baseline's access to the realized 30-day traffic drop, a signal deliberately withheld from the model's feature contract. Rather than discard that negative result, the two signals are combined into a three-tier action queue, where pages both approaches agree on show an 84.5% real decline rate against a 62% base rate in the test set. The deliverable is a decision-support ranking, not a causal or algorithmic claim: it tells an editorial team where to look first, not why any individual page's rankings moved.

02

Introduction & Problem Statement

Content teams face a structural bandwidth problem: creating new content is expensive, but so is manually auditing every published page for silent traffic decay. Search visibility fades gradually — a page can lose half its impressions over months before anyone on the editorial team notices, by which point recovering the position is far harder than catching the drop early.

This paper supports one specific decision: which published web pages should the editorial team review and update first, to prevent further loss of organic search traffic? The people who act on it are content managers, SEO strategists, and editorial copywriters working through a fixed weekly review capacity.

The cost of getting it wrong

  • False positive — flagging a healthy page as urgent wastes scarce editorial hours on content that didn't need attention.
  • False negative — missing a decaying page lets it keep bleeding rankings and impressions to competitors, unnoticed.

A fixed rule like "flag if age > 180 days" is a reasonable starting point, but it can't balance several weak, correlated signals — content age, prior traffic level, ranking position, page depth, and keyword demand — the way a model trained on historical outcomes can. This paper frames the lane as a scoring / ranking task (not classification or clustering): the output is a continuous priority score that lets a team work top-down through a sorted queue, rather than a flat yes/no bucket.

Success metric, fixed before modeling began: Precision@50 — of the top 50 pages the score flags, what share are genuinely declining? Target: >85%, against a raw dataset base rate of 54.2%.

03

Data

Release: the FlyRank ML Internship anonymized content-refresh dataset (public-safe extract) — one row per published article (content_id), 30,000 rows spanning 32 anonymized client sites (client_id).

Time window: a single cross-sectional snapshot per article, built from up to 90 trailing days of search-performance data, with a prev_30d / last_30d split used only to derive the evaluation label.

What's included: on-page attributes (word count, content age, content type), search-demand signals (search volume, competition), and traffic/ranking signals (impressions, average position, click-through rate, engagement) — every field anonymized, with no client names, domains, or raw URLs.

What was excluded, and why: trend_pct and trend_direction are excluded from the model's feature set — they're kept only as the evaluation label — because they're computed from the same window a forward-looking score would need to predict. Feeding them in as inputs is textbook feature leakage; a quick experiment confirmed it: including the leaked column pushed a test model to a suspicious 100% precision, which disappeared the moment the leaked field was removed.

04

Methodology

Features & label

Features (locked by a written data contract before modeling): content_age_days, impressions_prev_30d, avg_position, word_count, search_volume — all knowable at decision time, none derived from the outcome window.

Label: target_is_declining = 1 if trend_direction == 'down', else 0 — an observed historical outcome, not a subjective rating.

Baseline vs. model

Baseline (Week 4): a hand-tuned heuristic — flag pages older than 180 days whose last-30-day impressions dropped versus the prior 30 days, scored by drop_pct × log(1+age) × log(1+prior impressions).

Model: Logistic Regression (L2-regularized) on the 5 contract features, standardized. Chosen over a deeper ensemble because with only 5 predictors and ~20k usable rows, a linear model is the right complexity, and its coefficients translate directly into auditable reason codes for the editorial team — no black box.

Validation design

Split by GroupShuffleSplit, grouped by client (80/20), not by row. Rows from the same site share systemic factors — CMS, template, editorial cadence, niche — that a random row-level split would let leak between train and test, quietly inflating the score. Grouping means the model is tested on entirely unseen sites: 23 clients / 14,242 rows to train, 6 unseen clients / 5,776 rows to test, zero client overlap confirmed between the two.

Leakage checks performed: confirmed zero client overlap between train and test; confirmed the label fields (trend_direction, trend_pct, target_is_declining) never appear among model inputs; the baseline heuristic is re-scored on the exact same held-out test split as the model, for a fair, apples-to-apples comparison rather than two separately-tuned numbers.

Results

The honest headline: the baseline wins on this split. The heuristic has direct access to impressions_last_30d — the realized drop — while the 5-feature contract deliberately withholds that column so the model's score can be forward-looking rather than a lagging confirmation of a drop that already happened. Given only structural signals, the model's task is genuinely harder, and the numbers say so plainly. This is reported as the real result, not smoothed over.

Where the model goes wrong

Of the model's top-50 highest-risk picks, 24 were false positives — mostly young pages (~90–120 days old) with very low prior traffic (median 91 impressions), where low historical volume looks superficially like a page that has already collapsed. The model's worst misses run the other way: large, established pages (400+ days old, position 2–9, tens of thousands of prior impressions) that are declining, but whose accumulated authority currently suppresses the risk score — exactly the quiet-decay case this project exists to catch.

Standardized coefficients: content_age_days is the strongest single driver (negative — very old pages in this dataset skew evergreen and stable once prior traffic is held constant); word_count and avg_position push risk up slightly; impressions_prev_30d pushes it down; search_volume barely matters on its own.

06

Limitations & Honest Framing

  • No causal claim. Nothing here proves editing a page causes traffic to recover — that requires an A/B or staggered-rollout experiment, not an observational snapshot.
  • No algorithm exposure. This work does not reveal or approximate Google's ranking algorithm; it only associates on-page and historical performance signals with observed decline.
  • The model currently underperforms the simple rule on this test split, for the reason explained in Results. In its current form it should be read as directional and decision-support only, not a drop-in replacement for the heuristic.
  • False positives cluster among young, low-traffic pages — the model can't yet distinguish "never had much traffic" from "lost its traffic," since it has no trajectory feature.
  • Worst misses are large, established pages declining quietly — accumulated authority currently suppresses the model's risk score even when real decline is underway.
  • External shocks are invisible to this feature set — competitor campaigns, Google core updates, technical SEO issues, and seasonality are all indistinguishable here from genuine content decay.
  • Single cross-sectional snapshot, not a true time panel — a strictly time-aware split (in addition to the client-grouped one used here) was not possible with the data as provided.

Claims in this paper use careful language throughout: observed, measured, directional, decision-support — never proven, guaranteed, or causal.

07

Ranked Recommendations

Given the honest results above, the recommended playbook combines both signals rather than relying on the model alone. The test-set base rate — no filtering at all — is 62.1% decline; a useful tier should clear that bar.

Tier 1

Urgent refresh

988 pages

84.5% actual decline rate. Baseline rule and model both flag risk. Send to editorial today — the highest-confidence slice of the queue.

Tier 2

Secondary review

2,819 pages

66.9% actual decline rate. Model alone flags risk the rule would miss — a forward-looking catch, but noisier. Route to a human reviewer, not straight to urgent.

Tier 3

Routine / no action

1,969 pages

43.8% actual decline rate — near the dataset's overall churn, well below Tiers 1–2. Leave for periodic review.

Full ranked list (anonymized content_id, age, prior impressions, position, model score, and assigned tier) is exported as work/outputs/ranked_recommendations.csv in the repository — see Reproducibility.
